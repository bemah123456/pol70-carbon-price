What does $100 a ton of CO2 mean in actual life?

Carbon prices are quoted per metric ton of carbon dioxide. Nobody buys a ton of CO₂. You buy gallons, therms, kilowatt-hours, cement, steel and plane tickets. This page converts the price into those units using federal emission factors and this fall’s prices, then shows how the answer to “who pays?” flips depending on what happens to the money.

Each direct carbon charge on this page is calculated with this formula:

carbon charge per unit = kg of CO2 per unit ÷ 1,000 × price per ton

Burning a gallon of gasoline releases 8.887 kg of CO₂ (EPA). At $100 a ton: 8.887 ÷ 1,000 × $100 = $0.89 a gallon. The shopping-cart and who-pays charts are different: they rescale published economic models, explained under “How this was built.”

The same tax bites very differently

Cheap, carbon-heavy things jump the most. A metric ton of cement sells for about $160 but releases about 0.78 tons of CO₂ in the kiln, so at $100 its price rises 49%. Wholesale natural gas rises 155% because it is so cheap per unit of carbon. Gasoline is already expensive, so the same tax adds 20%.

Flying is priced per passenger

EPA’s travel factors divide a plane’s fuel burn across its passengers. Short hops burn more fuel per mile because takeoff and climb dominate.

Round trips, average seat, using EPA’s CO₂-only factors per passenger-mile (2025 edition: 0.207 short haul, 0.129 medium haul, 0.163 long haul). Distances are great-circle miles between the airports; flown routes are somewhat longer. These factors leave out contrails and other high-altitude effects that add warming beyond the CO₂. Treasury’s sample carbon tax design would exempt fuel for international flights, so the London trip might carry no charge at all unless Congress chose otherwise.

Electricity depends on who made it

A coal plant releases about 2.3 pounds of CO₂ per kilowatt-hour; a modern gas combined-cycle plant about 0.84; nuclear, wind, solar and hydro release none at the plant. At $100, that is the difference between 10.5¢ and nothing on every kilowatt-hour, against an average U.S. home price of 17.3¢ in 2025.

Carbon charge in cents per kWh at your price. Residential price: EIA 2025 annual average (Monthly Energy Review, Table 9.8). Coal, oil, gas fleet and grid averages are 2023 actuals from EIA; the two gas plant types are computed from EIA heat rates (7,146 and about 10,000 Btu per kWh) times EPA’s 53.06 kg CO₂ per million Btu.

The grid average understates what happens in power markets. In most wholesale markets the price is set by the last plant needed to meet demand, which is often gas. A carbon price lifts that price for everyone, so zero-carbon plants earn more while coal plants see their costs rise the most. That reshuffles which plants run first without anyone picking winners.

Through the shopping cart

Most of what households buy isn’t energy. Treasury’s Office of Tax Analysis traced a carbon tax through 389 industries to 33 consumer spending categories. Energy bills jump. Nearly everything else rises far less, because energy is a small slice of what it costs to make most things.

Treasury’s estimates at $49 a ton in 2019 dollars (about $64 today), scaled in proportion to your price, before Treasury’s adjustment that holds the overall price level fixed. Percentages are relative to 2019 prices; today’s higher energy prices would make the energy bars somewhat smaller.

Who pays depends on where the money goes

The U.S. released 4.90 billion metric tons of energy-related CO₂ in 2025 (EIA). Taxing all of it raises a lot of money, and what Congress does with that money decides who comes out ahead.

Example 1: split the money equally among everyone

4.90 billion tons× $100= $490 billion a year→ minus about 25% lost from other taxes= $368 billion÷ 341.8 million people= $1,075 per person

That is $4,300 for a family of four if every person, adult or child, got the same share. Static math: in reality emissions fall as people respond, so revenue shrinks over time. Treasury projected a $49-and-rising tax would cut covered emissions about 21% below baseline within five years.

How I used AI

I claimed this topic from the class list and asked Claude (Anthropic) to research it and build the exhibit. The following steps were completed:

  1. Searched for official CO₂ emission factors for each item in the topic, preferring federal sources (EPA, EIA) or the industry’s own reporting body (worldsteel).
  2. Pulled reference prices: EIA weekly fuel prices (September 2026), EIA 2025 annual electricity and natural gas prices, USGS’s 2025 cement estimate, and a mid-September 2026 U.S. steel price.
  3. Converted gas-plant heat rates into kg of CO₂ per kWh (heat rate in million Btu per MWh × 53.06 kg per million Btu) and converted EIA’s pounds into kilograms.
  4. Found published studies that model the same carbon tax with different uses of the revenue: Treasury’s 2017 methodology paper and the Tax Policy Center’s 2018 study.
  5. Checked whether TPC’s results scale with the tax rate. Across their $14, $50 and $73 runs the effects are close to proportional, so a straight line through the origin fits well. Their $73 (2016 dollars) is about $102 in today’s dollars, so $100 sits just inside their highest scenario.
  6. Wrote the page as a single interactive file with one price slider driving every chart, and listed every input in the tables in this section.
  7. After review, matched the flight and steel factors to their cited editions, relabeled reference prices with their dates, separated the two rebate examples, and documented the model inputs.

Kicking the tires

  • Who really pays. The price-tag charts assume full pass-through to buyers. In reality the burden splits among consumers, workers and owners, depending on how responsive each side is. Treasury and TPC model this explicitly; the price tags don’t.
  • Behavior. Everything is a snapshot before anyone adjusts. Utilities would run less coal, people would drive a bit less, and both the costs and the revenue would shrink over time.
  • CO₂ only. Methane leaks from gas systems and non-CO₂ effects from aviation are left out, which flatters natural gas and flying.
  • Averages hide plants. Individual coal plants, gas turbines and cement kilns vary a lot from the fleet averages used here.
  • Trade. Cement and steel compete with imports. Without a border adjustment, a U.S.-only carbon price could shift production abroad rather than cut emissions.
  • Prices move. Gasoline is about a dollar higher than a year ago, which makes the carbon charge a smaller percentage than it would have been in 2025.
  • The distribution study is dated. TPC modeled 2020 incomes under 2018 tax law, and “government keeps it” shows no benefit because TPC doesn’t assign any value to lower deficits.

Next questions someone could ask

  • What if half the revenue went to equal checks and half to payroll tax cuts?
  • How much cheaper is the electricity charge in New York, whose grid is cleaner than the national average, than in a coal-heavy state?
  • How much would emissions actually fall at $100, and how fast would revenue shrink?
  • What would a border carbon adjustment add to the price of imported steel from China versus Canada?
  • Where does “$100” come from? How does the social cost of carbon change with the discount rate?
